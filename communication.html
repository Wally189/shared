Be clear enough that seniority does not blur the ask

Professional communication and stakeholder handling

Professional does not mean vague. A director should be able to tell within seconds what is needed, by when, why the date matters and what happens next.

The action-request standard

ACTION — what exactly do you need?
OWNER — who needs to do it?
DEADLINE — exact date and time.
REASON — why does that deadline exist?
NEXT STEP — what happens once it is done?
IF MISSED — what process consequence follows?
BLOCKER ROUTE — who should they contact if the deadline cannot be met?

GOV.UK communication guidance recommends putting important information first, giving clear instructions and including deadlines and consequences. Government functional communication standards emphasise clear, concise and consistent communication.

Writing to directors and senior officers

  • Put the requested decision/action in the first sentence.
  • Use exact dates: “by 12:00 on Friday 2 October 2026”, not “ASAP” or “by Friday”.
  • Give one sentence on why the deadline matters: publication, clearance, chair briefing, statutory/local timetable.
  • Separate information from action. Do not make the reader hunt through background to find the ask.
  • Do not apologise for a legitimate governance requirement.
  • Do not overstate your own authority. State the process and source.
  • Give a route for early warning if the deadline cannot be met.

The “psychological war” translated professionally

The useful part of psychology is understanding incentives, friction, status, confidence and timing. The unsafe/unprofessional part is covert manipulation or amateur mind-reading.

Build a stakeholder plan from observable facts:

  • Authority: what can this person actually decide or block?
  • Dependency: what do you need from them, and what do they need from you?
  • Impact: what changes for them?
  • Friction: time, skill, duplication, uncertainty, perceived loss of control?
  • Evidence: what would make the case credible?
  • Channel: email, briefing, short call, demo, formal paper?
  • Timing: when are they most able to act?
  • Escalation: what is the legitimate route if the process is at risk?

Internal colleagues

Protect dignity. Correct privately where possible. Let experienced sceptics test edge cases. Make the new route easier than the workaround. Do not publicly compare digital competence or diagnose colleagues as insecure, difficult or technophobic.

External stakeholders

Assume correspondence may be forwarded or disclosed. Keep the record factual. State authority, requested action, exact date, consequence/next step and contact route. Avoid unnecessary emotion, threats or invented urgency.

Influence rules

  • Make the required behaviour easy.
  • Use evidence and process rather than status contests.
  • Offer bounded choices where real choices exist.
  • Use social proof only if it is true.
  • Never fake a deadline or consequence.
  • Never hide a material change from people affected by it.
  • Do not exploit personal vulnerabilities.
