Change the friction before you change the identity

Quiet modernisation

A messy system that works is not a failed system. It is usually a mixture of sound professional practice, accumulated workarounds, duplicated state, visual folklore and individual memory. Modernisation should preserve what works, expose what is hidden and remove friction gradually enough that people experience the change as help rather than conquest.

Psychology, not manipulation

“Subtle” should mean low-drama, respectful and easy to adopt—not deceptive. Do not manufacture consensus, hide consequences, shame people for low digital confidence or make an old route deliberately unusable before a replacement is proven. Behavioural insight is useful when it makes the better route easier, clearer and timely while leaving people informed about what is changing.

Transferable practice The Behavioural Insights Team's EAST framework summarises behaviour design as Easy, Attractive, Social and Timely. GOV.UK assisted-digital guidance separately says to understand why people are not adopting a digital route and provide support rather than simply forcing them online.

1. Respect the current system before touching it

Assume every oddity may have a reason until checked. A fluorescent cell, red wording, a duplicated tab or a strangely named file might be pointless—or it might encode an exception nobody documented. Ask:

  • Who uses this?
  • What decision or action does it support?
  • What does each colour, abbreviation or free-text convention mean?
  • Which formulas, hidden sheets, macros or linked workbooks exist?
  • Who consumes the output?
  • What breaks if it disappears tomorrow?

Use the Legacy System Inventory.

Colour is allowed as presentation; it should not be the database. WCAG 2.2 says colour must not be the only visual means of conveying information.

3. Triage spreadsheets before replacing them

Classify each workbook or tracker:

CANONICAL — currently the accepted record
WORKING — personal/team work in progress
EXPORT — generated from another authoritative system
DUPLICATE — repeats another source with no independent purpose
HISTORIC — retained for evidence/reference
UNKNOWN — purpose or ownership not yet understood

Do not migrate an UNKNOWN workbook simply because it exists. Do not delete one until dependencies are understood.

4. Reduce free text by design

Instructions such as “please only type Red/Amber/Green in this box” are weak controls. Where values are genuinely finite, use a Choice, Date, Person, Yes/No or other appropriate typed field. Keep free text for actual narrative. Microsoft Lists/SharePoint supports required fields, controlled choices and validation.

5. Keep the first replacement boringly familiar

The first version should often preserve familiar language, ordering and core workflow. Improve one thing—search, duplication, ownership, deadline visibility—without simultaneously changing terminology, hierarchy, colours, roles and meeting practice. A familiar-looking improvement creates less cognitive load and gives people evidence that change can help rather than erase their competence.

6. Treat resistance as diagnostic information

“I don't like it” can mean several different things:

Possible causeUseful response
Real process edge caseAsk the person to break the prototype; add the missing case.
Loss of confidenceProvide side-by-side help and a one-page job aid.
Loss of control/statusGive the person a meaningful review/ownership role rather than dismissing their expertise.
Bad timing/workloadDelay the change or reduce its scope.
Habit onlyMake the new route simpler and let repeated convenience do the persuading.
Legitimate disagreementCompare evidence and outcomes; do not convert disagreement into a personality diagnosis.

A particularly sceptical experienced user can be an excellent red-team tester. They know where tidy diagrams fail in real life.

7. Use assisted adoption

Do not call it remedial training. Provide help at the point of need: “show me once”, a short screen-share, a one-page sequence, a trusted colleague sitting beside someone for their first use, and an obvious route back to help. GOV.UK digital take-up guidance emphasises understanding reluctance and supporting digital confidence rather than assuming everybody can or should switch unaided.

8. Run old and new together only with an exit rule

Parallel running is useful for proving accuracy. Permanent parallel running is duplication. Before a pilot starts, define:

  • what is being compared;
  • how long the parallel period lasts;
  • what constitutes a material mismatch;
  • who decides the replacement is safe;
  • what happens to the old tracker afterwards;
  • how rollback works if the new method fails.

GOV.UK legacy guidance similarly recommends involving affected stakeholders, realistic prototypes and an agreed changeover rather than indefinite coexistence.

9. Make the better behaviour the easier behaviour

Prefer a single current link over emailed attachments; a pre-filtered view over hunting through tabs; a dropdown over memorising acceptable wording; an automatic date field over typing the same date twice; a stable document name over guessing which “final” is final. The psychological strategy is not persuasion theatre. It is removing reasons to prefer the old workaround.

10. Do not lead with AI

If AI helps privately with research, checking or drafting within policy, fine. The team-visible improvement should usually be described in ordinary operational terms: fewer duplicate entries, one current version, clear ownership, easier handover. Staff do not need to buy into an AI strategy to benefit from a better process.

Success measures

Measure less duplicate entry, fewer corrections, faster retrieval, fewer “which version?” questions, fewer uncontrolled values, fewer steps requiring one person's memory, adoption without repeated support, and whether the new method survives absence/leave. Do not measure success by how many spreadsheets were deleted or how many staff used AI.
