Worked example — fictional authority

One decision, end to end

A service wants approval for a new policy. That sentence sounds simple. Democratic Services sees the chain of authority, evidence, people, dates and records that must hold together before “approved” means anything useful.

This case is deliberately generic. “Northbridge Council”, its policy and its route are fictional. The example teaches questions and dependencies, not a universal council procedure. A real authority's constitution, delegation, access-to-information rules, report template and professional instructions govern.

By the end

You should be able to trace a proposal through a democratic decision journey, identify where authority and professional advice matter, distinguish discussion from formal decision, and explain why the record and follow-up are part of the same system.

The case

Northbridge Council's Community Services team proposes a revised Community Grants Policy. The report owner wants the new policy in force next quarter. They believe a committee must approve it, Finance has commented on the cost assumptions, and Legal advice is still being finalised.

01

Define the decision

Before booking a meeting slot, establish what approval is actually sought. Is the decision to approve the policy, recommend it elsewhere, consult on a draft, or note work already authorised?

Professional question: could an uninvolved reader state the decision requested in one sentence?

02

Establish authority and route

Check the applicable legal framework and Northbridge's current constitution, terms of reference and delegation. The fact that another council sends a similar policy to Cabinet proves nothing about Northbridge.

Stop condition: if the delegation or route cannot be established, do not invent it. Refer to the competent local governance/legal route.

03

Plan backwards

Once the decision-maker and meeting are known, work backwards through the local timetable: report completion, professional inputs, governance check, agenda preparation, publication and member briefing where applicable. Separate statutory requirements from local target dates.

04

Build a decision-ready report

The report owner owns the substantive proposal. Democratic Services checks the paper as a decision instrument: recommendations, authority, dates, figures, appendices, previous decisions and evidenced dependencies must cohere. Specialist issues go to the specialist owner.

05

Do not manufacture clearance

Finance has commented; Legal is still finalising advice. Those are different states. “Finance commented” does not mean “all clearances complete”, and a previous Legal view does not automatically clear today's proposition.

06

Agenda and publication

Only the applicable current rules establish what must be published, when, in what form and with what treatment for information that may lawfully be withheld. Accessibility and intelligibility matter alongside timing.

07

The meeting is a decision point, not the whole process

Members receive the proposal through the lawful route, hear the material presented under the applicable procedure, debate and may move amendments. Officers support the process and provide professional advice within their roles; they do not substitute their political preference for the members' decision.

08

Capture what was actually decided

Discussion, officer advice, a proposal, an amendment and the formal decision are not interchangeable. The formal record must be supported by what happened and follow any prescribed local form. Minutes are proportionate records, not imaginative reconstructions or automatic transcripts.

09

Turn the decision into follow-through

Record actions, owners and dates where evidenced; complete any required decision notice or publication; and ensure the authoritative record can be retrieved. The policy team then implements within the authority actually granted.

10

Make it institutional memory

Months later, a new officer should be able to establish what was decided, by whom, under what authority, from which report and when it took effect—without relying on the memory of the person who serviced the meeting.

Try the difficult moment

It is publication morning. Finance has commented. Legal advice remains outstanding. The report owner says the recommendations have not changed and asks Democratic Services to publish anyway.

Before revealing the check: What do you know? What is unknown? Who owns each judgement? What local rule or instruction matters? What must you not assume?

Reveal the professional check

You know only the stated facts. You do not know whether Legal clearance is required, whether it is outstanding in a way that blocks publication, or who has authority to accept that risk. Check the current local process and competent professional owner. Democratic Services can make the dependency and deadline visible; it should not invent Legal approval or a universal publication rule.

Why this matters

A weak service treats each artefact separately: forward plan, report, agenda, minutes, action tracker. A stronger service sees one connected decision journey. That makes failure easier to diagnose: late papers may be a planning problem, repeated amendments may be a report-development problem, and missing actions may be a record-to-follow-up problem.
