The human operating system

Run a reliable Democratic Services function

A service can have a beautiful constitution and still be fragile if deadlines live in one person's head, nobody can cover a meeting, quality checks happen only after publication, or colleagues cannot tell who owns the next step. Reliability is a property of the service, not heroism by individual officers.

Boundary

This page teaches diagnostic principles, not an employment model. It does not prescribe staffing numbers, grades, job descriptions, performance management or recruitment. Those belong to the organisation's management authority, HR framework, employment law and local circumstances.

Seven questions for the service

1. What are we here to make reliable?

Define the service mandate in outcomes: lawful and intelligible decision routes, prepared members and chairs, ready papers, dependable meetings, accurate records, transparency, follow-through and durable knowledge.

2. Who owns what?

Make responsibilities and professional interfaces intelligible. Report owners, Democratic Services, Legal/Monitoring Officer, Finance/S151 and other specialists should not be blurred into one generic “governance” owner.

3. Can the workload be seen?

Forward work, meeting cycles, paper dependencies, recurring appointments and known peaks should be visible early enough to manage. A service cannot sensibly discuss capacity if demand is invisible.

4. Can somebody else pick it up?

Core work needs enough documented method, accessible records, handover and cross-cover that an absence or departure does not erase the operating system.

5. How is quality caught?

Define proportionate checks before the point of failure: decision readiness, agenda completeness, accessibility, formal-record accuracy and action follow-through. Quality assurance should find defects, not merely count activity.

6. Are people equipped for their roles?

Induction and continuing development should be tied to real tasks and responsibilities. Current LGA material treats councillor and officer development as important to skills and capacity, and specifically identifies committee and chair development as an area in which Democratic Services may contribute.

7. Does the service learn?

When something nearly fails, investigate the process and context rather than relying on another reminder to “be more careful”. Preserve legitimate controls, correct the defect, test the change and share the learning proportionately.

Capacity is not just headcount

Capacity is the relationship between demand, timing, skills, dependencies, cover and available resource. Two teams with the same number of officers can face very different loads because their governance structures, meeting patterns, member-support expectations, systems and local procedures differ.

Useful diagnostic questions include: Which cycles collide? Which work has immovable dates? Which tasks require scarce specialist knowledge? Where does rework consume time? Which responsibilities have no cover? Which “urgent” requests are predictable if forward planning improves?

Relationships are part of the control environment

Democratic Services sits between elected members, report owners, senior officers, specialist advisers and the public. Clear professional relationships reduce ambiguity about authority, deadlines and expectations. Scrutiny practice also depends on constructive relationships and sufficient resources; current CfGS guidance treats structures, resources, formal powers and prioritisation as connected conditions for effective scrutiny.

Induction should reconstruct the system

A new starter should not merely receive a folder of procedures. They should be able to explain the bodies they support, find the current source of authority, trace one decision, identify recurring deadlines, locate authoritative records, understand escalation routes and know which questions require another professional owner.

Diagnostic exercise: imagine the most experienced officer is unexpectedly absent for two weeks. Pick one committee. Can another competent officer establish its powers, next meeting, live papers, deadlines, member/chair arrangements, outstanding actions and authoritative records without private messages or oral folklore?

If not, record the dependency before inventing a new system. The missing capability might be documentation, access, forward planning, cross-training, workload or several of these.

What good looks like

Good is not “everybody is busy” or “the meetings happened”. Look for reproducible outcomes: fewer avoidable late surprises, clearer ownership, first-pass paper readiness, accurate records, retrievable decisions, resilient cover, effective member support, reduced rework and evidence that improvements actually helped.

Evidence position

Professional/sector practice: current LGA material supports deliberate councillor and officer development and identifies Democratic Services' role in committee/chair induction. Current CfGS practice guidance links effective scrutiny with structures, resources, powers, prioritisation and relationships. The seven-question service model above is an author synthesis for diagnosis; it is not a statutory or nationally prescribed Democratic Services operating model.

Independent and unofficial. Local management authority, HR policy, employment law, organisational structure and professional judgement govern real staffing and people decisions.
