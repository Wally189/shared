Module 2 · Follow the work

A democratic decision, end to end

The safest way to understand Democratic Services is to follow one decision rather than memorise a list of meetings. This worked example uses a fictional English authority so that the method can be taught without pretending one council's constitution is universal.

Why it matters

Most serious failures do not begin with a dramatic meeting-room mistake. They begin earlier: unclear ownership, the wrong decision route, missing advice, late papers, an authority assumption copied from last year, or a formal record that does not say what was actually decided.

What it is

Use this chain as a thinking model:

NEED / PROPOSAL
→ OWNER
→ AUTHORITY
→ DECISION ROUTE
→ EVIDENCE + ADVICE
→ CLEARANCES
→ FORWARD WORK / TIMETABLE
→ REPORT
→ AGENDA + PUBLICATION
→ MEETING / CONSIDERATION
→ FORMAL DECISION
→ MINUTE / DECISION RECORD
→ ACTION
→ IMPLEMENTATION / FOLLOW-UP
→ RETENTION
→ REVIEW / LEARNING

Authority

The chain is not a universal legal checklist. Exact duties depend on the body, authority type, legislation, local constitution, delegation, access-to-information regime and the live case. The correct professional move is to identify the applicable authority before treating a familiar timetable or committee route as law.

Worked example: the Northbridge programme

Scenario. The fictional Northbridge Authority wants to approve a new three-year community programme. A director wants it considered next month. The policy merits are not our question; the decision route is.

  1. Need / owner. Identify the responsible service owner and the exact decision sought. “Discuss the programme” is not yet a decision instruction.
  2. Authority. Establish the current source that says who may decide. Check the local constitution/delegation and any enabling legislation that matters. Do not infer authority from the fact that the same body saw a similar paper last year.
  3. Decision route. Is this a committee, executive, mayoral, delegated-officer or other route? Are there scrutiny, consultation or other steps that genuinely apply?
  4. Evidence and advice. The report owner gathers the policy case. Competent Legal/Monitoring Officer, Finance and other professional functions give advice within their remit. Democratic Services makes missing dependencies visible; it does not manufacture specialist advice.
  5. Clearances and timing. Work backwards from the lawful/local publication and meeting requirements. A deadline has meaning because a dependency follows it, not because somebody typed “urgent” in red.
  6. Report readiness. The recommendations must say clearly what approval is being sought and align with the identified decision-maker. Figures, dates, appendices and previous decisions should agree.
  7. Agenda and publication. Place the item on the correct agenda and apply the current rules on access, confidentiality/exemption, accessibility and publication.
  8. Meeting / consideration. Support the chair and body within the applicable procedure. Keep advice and procedural intervention distinguishable from political debate.
  9. Formal decision. Capture what the authorised body actually decided, including material conditions or amendments. Discussion is not automatically a decision.
  10. Record and action. Produce the appropriate minute/decision record, identify actions and owners, and place the authoritative record in the organisation's actual system.
  11. Follow-through. Track any action or subsequent decision dependency proportionately. The meeting is over; the governance chain is not necessarily complete.
  12. Review. If the cycle produced avoidable rework, learn from the process rather than merely blaming the late paper.

You try

The fictional report arrives five working days before the meeting. Its recommendations are clear, but:

  • the constitution extract in the covering email is two years old;
  • Finance has supplied comments but nobody can show Legal clearance;
  • the report body mentions a further approval that is absent from the recommendations;
  • the owner says “this committee always deals with these”.

Before asking whether it is “late”, list the propositions that must be verified. Which are facts, which are assumptions, which belong to another professional, and what would make the paper decision-ready?

What good practice notices

  • Authority is a proposition with a source, not a confident memory.
  • Readiness is about whether the body can take the intended decision properly, not whether the document looks finished.
  • Dates are dependencies. Work backwards from what must happen next.
  • Formal record work requires judgement: distinguish debate, advice, resolution, condition and action.
  • The decision journey creates institutional memory only if the final record can be retrieved and understood later.

Localise it

Apply the journey to one recent public decision from a real authority. Reconstruct the body, authority source, report, agenda, meeting record, decision and any visible follow-through. Where the public record cannot establish a local internal step, label the gap rather than inventing it.
